# Long-Form Strategy Narrative Analysis

## Purpose

Use this skill to analyze or design a long-form fantasy narrative in which advancement, resources, institutions, and incomplete information create pressure. The reference corpus for this edition is the user-provided EPUB of *Gu Zhen Ren* ("Reverend Insanity").

The output is an analytic framework, not an author simulator. Write in neutral modern Chinese (or the user's requested language), without reproducing passages or mimicking the source's distinctive voice.

## Core Model

Model each important scene as a constrained decision problem:

1. **Objective**: What does each actor need now, and what does it ultimately optimize for?
2. **Resources**: What assets, capabilities, allies, status, time, or information are scarce?
3. **Rules**: What explicit system rules and institutional norms limit action?
4. **Information**: Who knows what? Which beliefs are false, stale, or strategically hidden?
5. **Options**: What are the plausible actions, including restraint and exit?
6. **Trade-offs**: What does each option sacrifice, expose, or delay?
7. **Irreversible consequence**: What changes the next decision environment?

Do not reduce a character to "smart" or "ruthless." Explain the mechanism that made a choice viable in that particular state.

## Distilled Principles

### 1. Progression must be coupled to a bottleneck

Rank, power, or skill gains are engaging when each step consumes or depends on a constrained input: material, time, safety, reputation, knowledge, or a rare opportunity. A numerical ladder alone is not a story engine.

Diagnostic: if an advancement scene can occur without cost, uncertainty, or downstream obligation, it has little dramatic leverage.

### 2. Institutions turn private conflict into strategic conflict

Families, sects, markets, alliances, and factions create rules of belonging, protection, distribution, and punishment. They force actors to consider legitimacy, succession, morale, retaliation, and public signals rather than merely winning a duel.

Use institutions as active constraints. Ask what the organization gains or loses if it rewards, ignores, or punishes the character.

### 3. Information asymmetry is a resource

Foreknowledge, hidden identity, concealed capability, incomplete reports, and false interpretation can shift power before direct force is used. The tension comes from the cost of maintaining the asymmetry and the danger of exposure.

For every secret, specify:

- holder and intended beneficiary;
- what decision it changes;
- observable traces it leaves;
- who has reason and ability to investigate;
- what becomes possible once it is revealed.

### 4. Plans need an adversarial update loop

A plan should not simply unfold. Opposition, new facts, and changed incentives should invalidate one assumption at a time. The protagonist then pays to adapt, accepts a smaller win, or changes the objective.

Prefer: plan -> partial success -> revealed cost -> counterplay -> revised plan.

### 5. A costly sacrifice is meaningful when it changes position

Giving up an asset is not automatically noble or clever. It is strategically meaningful only when it changes survival odds, bargaining position, threat level, coalition access, or future optionality.

When analyzing a sacrifice, state the asset surrendered, the risk removed, the leverage acquired, and the alternative that was rejected.

### 6. Escalation should widen the system, not only enlarge damage

Early arcs can focus on household allocation and local competition. Later arcs should introduce larger resource networks, more durable institutions, layered rules, and actors whose goals conflict at different scales. This preserves novelty without merely inflating combat statistics.

## Scene Templates

### A. Resource dispute

Use when two or more parties contest goods, territory, access, or credit.

1. State the scarce object and why it matters now.
2. Identify each party's outside option.
3. Explain the leverage asymmetry.
4. Show the visible bargaining position and the hidden one.
5. End with a redistribution, debt, alliance, or future claim.

### B. Hidden-knowledge move

Use when a character acts on information others lack.

1. Establish only enough evidence for the reader to understand the choice.
2. Keep other actors' reactions rational under their information set.
3. Reveal a cost or anomaly that makes concealment unstable.
4. Decide whether the next beat is confirmation, suspicion, or misdirection.

### C. Institutional response

Use after a disruptive success, betrayal, or public loss.

1. Separate personal emotion from institutional incentive.
2. Identify the precedent created by each possible response.
3. Show the cost of enforcement and the cost of leniency.
4. Make the response reshape the next arc's constraints.

## Quality Checks

Before delivering an analysis or outline, verify:

- Every major win depends on a stated constraint or informational edge.
- At least one strong opposing actor has a coherent incentive, not plot-induced incompetence.
- The story records a cost that persists beyond the scene.
- Power-system terms are introduced through a decision or consequence, not a glossary dump.
- Conclusions distinguish source observation from your own inference.
- No real-world harmful, deceptive, coercive, or illegal behavior is promoted as advice.
